01 · Before the log

A data analytics student, a spreadsheet habit, and no backtest.

Reading note: this isn't a trading-strategy pitch — there's no system or edge on offer, and the account still failed. It's a decision-hygiene case study: what an honestly-logged, two-month dataset actually says about how choices degrade under uncertainty.

I went into this with a Data Analytics degree in progress and a habit I didn't think much of at the time: logging everything. Not a trading habit — a student one. It happened to be the only discipline I brought into the account.

Everything else was closer to "figure it out live." No backtest. No demo run first — the one demo session on record has me sizing 10 lots on EUR/USD by mistake. Two AI copilots (Claude, Gemini) sat in the chat window through most sessions, disagreed with each other constantly, and were overruled by me about as often as they were right.

03 · The part that isn't a clean story

The checklist lost money. The gut calls didn't. That's not the lesson either.

If the story were "trust your gut, checklists don't work," there'd be a clean trade to point at and say see. There isn't. The cleanest counter-example in the set runs the other way entirely:

Day 13 · Buy EUR/USD · 0.35 lots

Full checklist. EMA-aligned entry, RSI in range, R:R 1 : 1.51, stop placed under the deepest EMA-50 low, no chasing. Every box ticked — graded A, 5 out of 5, before the outcome was known. It still lost $38.50, because volatility roughly doubled mid-trade in a way no entry-time checklist is built to see.

5/5 checklist scoreResult: loss

That's not a discipline failure — it's variance a rules-based system is supposed to absorb over a large enough sample, and 15 trades isn't that sample yet. Pull the full grading log and the pattern gets stranger, not simpler:

A perfect checklist score didn't reliably beat a near-perfect one, and a near-perfect one didn't reliably beat a bad one. Score predicted process, not outcome.

04 · Same style, opposite outcome

Day 54 vs. Day 55 — both fully discretionary. Both broke from checklist. One made money.

This pair is the comparison that actually explains the gap, because "technical vs. feel" doesn't. Hover a point on either line for that trade.

Day 54 — 6 trades, mixed direction, +$190.40 Day 55 — 10 trades, all Sell, –$555.30

Day 54: bought a dip, sold a top, cut a losing short quickly when the market disagreed, let a winner run to +$239. Six trades, six fresh reads of the tape.

Day 55: every trade sold the same thesis into a confirmed uptrend. Lot size never shrank after a loss — it held flat, then crept up, then tripled on the last one: 0.09 → 0.09 → 0.09 → 0.09 → 0.09 → 0.10 → 0.12 → 0.10 → 0.10 → 0.30. The market said no ten times. The position size never heard it.

Same trader, same instrument, same absence of a checklist. The variable isn't technical-vs-feel — it's whether each trade updates on the last one, or repeats it.

05 · What actually broke

The interface was misleading. That's not the same as being confused.

Two missed fills, one tick above the exact top, triggered the size-up — then it ran Day 55's pattern straight to the end: same direction, same thesis, size never shrinking. The whole time, the number I kept glancing at was Net — closed trades only, sitting calm. The number a daily-loss breach actually reads is equity: floating positions included. That gap is real, it's in the log, it's worth a tool. But on its own, it's not the reason — and I don't think it's even the main one.

By the time size started climbing, I'd already worked the real number out by hand — roughly –$436, about $64 from the $500 line — and had already gone hunting in a trading community for what time the daily counter resets (4 a.m.). Neither of those is what someone misreading a column does. What gave out wasn't the arithmetic — it was whether the thing on the other side of it still felt worth protecting. A $500 daily allowance, guarding an 8% target (~$800) that's genuinely hard to hit in a month (plenty of good discretionary traders are happy with 1.5–2%), against a $78 entry fee and a month of watching charts — stops looking like much, somewhere in there.

The payout had quietly stopped feeling big enough to defend.Defending it was the entire job.

The two missed fills were the spark, not the cause — landing 1–2 points above the exact top, on an otherwise-correct read, feels unbearable in the moment and means nothing a week later. The final trade, 0.3 lots against nine straight losing sells, wasn't a miscalculation. Looking back, it reads like a decision that had already been made — the size just made it official.

I'm still not fully sure I trust my own read of that night. I can point to the numbers I calculated and the times I looked up; I can't point to a clean account of what I was thinking while I did it — and I'd rather say that than paper over it. What I am confident of: this wasn't a comprehension failure. "Wrong column, case closed" would've been the tidier story. It also would've been wrong.

The two rules this dataset actually supports RULE 1 — not "be more disciplined." That's true of almost every trading loss ever and explains none of them specifically.

IF N consecutive losses on an unchanged thesis
THEN stop and re-verify the actual risk number before the N+1st trade
— regardless of whether the approach that day is rules-based or discretionary. Both styles can fail this test; both did, at different points in this same dataset.

RULE 2 — the one the first rule doesn't cover.

IF the payoff has quietly stopped feeling worth the guard-rail protecting it
THEN that's the exit signal, decided before the session, not the moment size goes up until it feels worth it again.
